01 / A life in motion

The body has always
been the starting point.

Movement is something I study, something I build around, and something I practice. Exercise physiology gave me a way to understand how the body responds and adapts. A lifetime of training keeps that understanding personal.

Standing beside my son at HYROX. Traveling with my wife. Staying capable of saying yes to what comes next. Those are the reasons strength, health, and longevity matter to me.

That same curiosity has shaped my career: how can we help people move, feel, and live better—and make the experience something they want to return to?

02 / What the years have taught me

A wider view.
Built through experience.

My work has taken me from understanding human movement to understanding the products, people, and businesses that surround it.

Working in fitness equipment and product development, including at Technogym, meant thinking about the point where a person meets a machine. Building Functional Solutions brought connected fitness and digital coaching into that picture. Each experience added another way to see the same challenge.

Through Hannum Strategy and Fire and Ice Wellness, those questions remain close to everyday life. A product has to make sense to the person using it. A service has to earn someone’s trust. A business has to deliver on what it promises.

That combination shapes my judgment. Physiology helps me understand the body. Product experience helps me understand the interaction. Building and operating businesses keeps me grounded in what people will actually use.

03 / What brings it all together

A lifetime of experience.
A different way forward.

I’ve spent my life working with the body, physical products, and the businesses built around helping people. Now those worlds can connect in ways that fit more naturally into everyday life.

A sensor captures activity. A conversation captures how someone feels. Intelligence brings that information together with what we understand about the individual. The experience might live in a care professional’s dashboard, a coaching conversation, or a device already in someone’s life.

I think of software increasingly like electricity: something we expect to be there, making things work. What matters is what we connect, how we interpret it, and what it enables a person to do. That is where my experience in physiology, products, and building businesses comes together.

04 / Where the physical and digital meet

Real-time data.
Understanding in the moment.

Sensors, wearables, devices, and machines connect us to what is happening now. The opportunity is to interpret that data as it arrives—bringing physiology, intelligence, and personal context together to help someone decide what to do next.

Capture→Interpret→Respond
01Capture→

What’s happening now.

Sensors & devices

Signals such as heart rate and cadence help describe the body’s response and movement as they happen.

Wearables

Available live measurements connect the person to the moment. Activity and recovery history add context.

Machines & equipment

Treadmills, bikes, rowers, and connected equipment capture the work: speed, distance, cadence, or power.

02Interpret→

What it means.

Bring incoming data together with the person’s history, goals, and how they feel.

The aim is to interpret changes as they happen: how the body is responding, how effort is changing, and what deserves attention.

A by AMC · Audiotherm development concept

The same thinking.
In the kitchen.

In my work on the AMC cooking concept, I’m asking the same question I ask in fitness: how can a physical product help someone understand what is happening and know what to do next? Here, that thinking becomes a sensor in the lid and a clear cue for the cook.

Understanding at the product.

The lid works out the cooking state. The ring communicates it through a simple visual cue. “Now” means a cooking window; the cook still checks the food and adjusts the heat.

One signal. Optional connections.

The same state and method could reach a countertop controller, an app, or compatible partner devices. The standalone ring remains the basic experience.

Development concept. Optional connections require compatibility and safety testing; products and partner integrations are not confirmed.

Where I see the market heading

Software becomes the utility.
Understanding becomes the value.

I believe software creation is becoming a commodity. It will get easier to build exactly the experience you want. The app store as we know it will change, and more of the value will sit in what businesses make available: connections to devices, meaningful data, and proprietary intelligence.

Apps in ChatGPT and interactive tools in Claude are early examples of services reaching people through another interface. I see businesses increasingly selling access to capabilities that can work together, with software making the connections.

HearthActivity becomes individual patterns that care professionals can use in their work.
VantageA coaching conversation becomes a way to bring someone’s history and goals into the next decision.
MindDocketAn idea captured in one place becomes something to explore with AI—or a workout or recipe to put into practice.

I think about adoption through an 80/20 lens: a technically confident minority will build and connect their own systems. Most people will want to describe what they need and use something that already works. That is my working view, not a measured market split.

For that majority, the opportunity is to deliver a complete, dependable experience: the right connections, useful intelligence, and an interface that fits their day. A care professional needs a clear view of their clients. Someone training needs guidance they can act on. Neither should have to become a software builder.

This is what today’s tools make possible for me: bringing a lifetime of experience into products people can use. My contribution is knowing what to connect, what it means, and how it should feel in someone’s life.
